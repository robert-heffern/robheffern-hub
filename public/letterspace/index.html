Letterspace

Every letter is a place. Here's the map — and you can walk around in it.

Here's a strange idea that turns out to be true. When a computer learns what letters look like, it doesn't store them as pictures in a drawer. It arranges them in a kind of space — the way towns are arranged on a map. Every letter gets a location. Letters that look alike end up near each other; letters that look different end up far apart. And the empty land between the letters isn't empty at all — it's full of shapes that are half one letter and half another.

That probably sounds abstract. So let's not explain it — let's just go there. Below is the actual map a small AI built. Drag the dot around and watch what happens.

Walk around the map

The faint letters are real letters, sitting at their real locations. Grab the dot and drag it. The big letter on the right is whatever shape lives at that exact spot — drag toward a letter and it becomes that letter; stop in between two and you get the thing that's halfway between them. There's no list being picked from. Every point is a shape, and you're walking through all of them.

What you were actually doing

You were moving a point through the AI's imagination and asking, at every step, "what shape is here?" The model answers by painting — it was never choosing from the 26 letters it knows. It genuinely builds the shape at wherever you point, including the in-between places no real letter occupies. Those halfway shapes are the tell: a lookup table can't do that. A space can.

Why would anyone build it this way? Because a space lets you do arithmetic with shapes. That's the part that feels like magic, and it's next.

The straight line between two letters

Pick a letter to start and a letter to end. The slider walks in a straight line from one to the other, through the space. Watch the shape hand off from the first to the second — and notice it stays letter-ish the whole way. That's the space being smooth: no cliffs, no teleporting, just one shape easing into another.

A direction you can walk — the same everywhere

Here's the single idea that makes this powerful, and it needs no math. On a real map, "go north" is a direction that means the same thing wherever you're standing. Walk north from your house or north from the shop — it's the same move. A direction like that, one you can apply from anywhere, is what people mean by the word vector. That's the whole definition: a vector is just a reusable "go this way, this far."

Now the surprising bit. In letterspace there is a single direction that means "make this a capital." One fixed heading. Walk it from c and you arrive at C. Walk the same heading from o and you land on O; from w you get W. The model was never taught this arrow — it fell out of how the letters arranged themselves. Start the slider on c, o, v, w, or x and push — the small letter grows into its capital:

slide to walk the "make it a capital" direction

Then switch it to a, g, or e and push the same slider. It barely moves. That's not a glitch — it's the model telling you something true. C and c are the same drawing at two sizes, so one arrow gets you there. But A and a are different shapes we just agreed to call the same letter — there's no single move between them, so the arrow stalls. You can feel, with a slider, which of our letters are really one shape and which are two in a trenchcoat.

The working version is the shape cousin of a famous word trick, where a computer does king − man + woman and lands on queen — the same reusable-direction idea, done with letter shapes instead of word meanings.

Some things are directions. Some things are journeys.

You might guess the alphabet works the same way — that there's a "next letter" arrow you keep walking: A to B to C. It turns out there isn't. If you measure it, the step from A to B points one way, B to C points a slightly different way, C to D turns again. The alphabet isn't a straight heading you can reuse; it's a winding road. Casing is "go north." The alphabet is "follow the river." One is a vector; the other is a path that bends the whole way. Finding out which operations are straight and which are curved is a real thing people study — and you just felt the difference.

A dial the model invented on its own

Why this is the whole game

Everything you just did is, more or less, how modern AI works under the hood — not just for letters. A model that handles words, images, or sounds is doing the same move: it turns each thing into a location in a big space, arranges them so that similar things sit close, and then gets its powers from the geometry — the straight-line betweens, the reusable direction-arrows, the emergent dials. "Understanding," for a machine, largely means "having arranged things in a space where the directions mean something." You just walked around inside one.

This one only knows letters, and it's small enough to run live on your phone while you drag. But the shape of the trick is the same one running the big ones.
