Fixed Points

Every operation has shapes it refuses to change. They tell you what it really does.

Take any operation that turns one shape into another — make-it-a-capital, or step-to-the-next-letter. Most shapes, it moves. But almost every operation has a few special shapes it leaves exactly where they are. Push them through and nothing happens. Those are its fixed points, and they're worth hunting for, because the shapes an operation won't move turn out to be the clearest X-ray of what it's actually doing. Here are the fixed points of the two operations this little corner of the site is built on.

The letters that are their own capital

Run "make it a capital" across the alphabet and ask which letters don't really change. Here they are — top row small, bottom row capital. Look how little moves: these letters are the same drawing at two sizes.

Now the opposite end — the letters casing does move, hard. Top row small, bottom row capital, and they barely look related:

a g e r d h — two different shapes we agreed to call one letter

That split is the whole point. Half of our alphabet is one shape you resize. The other half is a pair of unrelated drawings — a lowercase and an uppercase that look nothing alike — that history glued together under one name. You never notice, because you learned them as children. The fixed points make it visible: casing is a real, simple operation for half the letters and a total costume change for the other half.

The one place every letter ends up

The other operation — step to the next glyph — has fixed points too, but of a stranger kind. Feed a letter its own output over and over (the heat-death trick from the other pages) and it drifts until it reaches a shape that steps to itself. That shape is a fixed point: succession can't move it any further. Every letter in a script falls into the same one.

Nine scripts (rows); six different starting letters each, run to exhaustion. Within a row they land in nearly the same place.

So succession has essentially one fixed point per alphabet — a single resting blob that the whole script pours into. It's not a letter. It's what's left when you apply "next" so many times that the idea of "which letter" wears off entirely. The casing fixed points are letters that were already done. The succession fixed point is the place letters go to stop being letters.

Why anyone cares about the things that don't move

This isn't just a curiosity. Finding the shapes an operation leaves alone is one of the oldest moves in mathematics — they're close cousins of what physicists and mathematicians call eigenvectors, and whole fields are built on hunting them down. The reason is exactly what you just saw: a thing can be complicated and hard to describe, but its fixed points are few, specific, and honest. They're the skeleton. Ask what an operation does to everything and you drown. Ask what it leaves alone and it tells you its secret.
