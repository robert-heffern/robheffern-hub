Around the year 400, a man in Rome got up, took a small token out of his clothing, walked to the public ovens, and climbed a particular flight of steps. Not any flight. His.

Edward Gibbon describes the arrangement with the flatness of a clerk reading out a schedule:

> For the convenience of the lazy plebeians, the monthly distributions of corn were converted into a daily allowance of bread; a great number of ovens were constructed and maintained at the public expense; and at the appointed hour, each citizen, who was furnished with a ticket, ascended the flight of steps which had been assigned to his peculiar quarter or division, and received, either as a gift or at a very low price, a loaf of bread of the weight of three pounds for the use of his family.

A three-pound loaf a day for the family, in Roman pounds, so call it a kilo. Bacon for five months of the year, which under Valentinian III came to more than three and a half million pounds annually. Oil taxed out of Africa by the shipload. Wine, once Augustus's grim little joke about the aqueducts had worn off. And the baths, which Gibbon cannot help admiring: sixteen hundred marble seats at Caracalla's, three thousand at Diocletian's, hot water pouring through "wide mouths of bright and massy silver," so that "the meanest Roman could purchase, with a small copper coin, the daily enjoyment of a scene of pomp and luxury which might excite the envy of the kings of Asia."

Then the sentence everybody remembers. "From these stately palaces issued a swarm of dirty and ragged plebeians, without shoes and without a mantle; who loitered away whole days in the street or Forum to hear news and to hold disputes; who dissipated in extravagant gaming the miserable pittance of their wives and children."

That is the picture we have all inherited. Free bread, a soft mob, a ruined city. It is the oldest cautionary tale in the Western argument about helping the poor, and most people can produce it from memory without ever having opened Gibbon.

The token is the strangest object in the passage, though. Rome could have handed out bread from a cart. Instead it built ovens, printed tickets, and assigned every man a staircase according to the quarter of the city he lived in. Before Rome fed him, Rome had to know where he belonged.

## The Argument Everyone Already Has

Ask people what the fight over poor relief is about and you get a version of the same answer, whichever side they take. It is about generosity against discipline. One camp says a decent society does not let people starve. The other says help has a habit of becoming a habit, and that a man who is supported stops supporting himself. The dial runs from hard to soft, everyone stands somewhere on it, and the thing being dialed up and down is money.

That framing is not a strawman put up to be knocked over. It is the frame the best minds in the tradition used, and some of them stated it better than we do.

Here is Tiberius in the Roman Senate in AD 16. A young nobleman named Marcus Hortalus had risen to ask the Senate for money to raise his four sons. Tiberius crushed him in terms that would pass unaltered in a modern newspaper column:

> If all poor men begin to come here and to beg money for their children, individuals will never be satisfied, and the State will be bankrupt... Otherwise industry will languish and idleness be encouraged, if a man has nothing to fear, nothing to hope from himself, and every one, in utter recklessness, will expect relief from others, thus becoming useless to himself and a burden to me.

Nothing to fear and nothing to hope from himself. Two thousand years later the argument has not found a better sentence.

Montesquieu puts the same thought as a definition: "A man is not poor because he has nothing, but because he does not work." In Rome, he observes, "the hospitals place every one at his ease except those who labour, except those who are industrious, except those who have land." He prefers emergencies to institutions, since the trouble is usually temporary and the cure should be too: "transient assistances are much better than perpetual foundations."

Aristotle had already supplied the image that the whole tradition reaches for. In democracies with money to spend, he warns, the leaders hand out the surplus, and it does no good: "the poor are always receiving and always wanting more and more, for such help is like water poured into a leaky cask."

Even Hegel, who is about to become this argument's most uncomfortable witness, concedes the core of it. What ruins a man is not the lack of money by itself. It is what happens next:

> When the standard of living of a large mass of people falls below a certain subsistence level... and when there is a consequent loss of the sense of right and wrong, of honesty and the self-respect which makes a man insist on maintaining himself by his own work and effort, the result is the creation of a rabble of paupers.

So the prior has real weight behind it. Feeding people may weaken them; the tradition has said so in five languages across two millennia; the argument is about how much to give and how careful to be. Anyone who has ever had this conversation at a dinner table has had it on these terms.

The laws said something else.

## Its Own Poor

The English poor law is the most documented experiment in the history of the subject. It ran for two hundred and thirty years in something like one shape, from 1601 to 1834, and Adam Smith took the trouble to write out its whole legislative history in *The Wealth of Nations*, calling it "this disorder, the greatest perhaps of any in the police of England."

It begins with one sentence. By the 43rd of Elizabeth, chapter 2, "every parish should be bound to provide for its own poor," with overseers appointed yearly to raise a parish rate for the purpose.

The word that caused two centuries of trouble was not *provide*. It was *own*.

Smith saw it immediately: "Who were to be considered as the poor of each parish became, therefore, a question of some importance." Every statute after 1601 is an answer to that question, and the answers have almost nothing to do with money.

Thirteen and fourteen Charles II: forty days' undisturbed residence gains you a settlement in a parish, but within those forty days two justices of the peace may remove you back to wherever you last legally belonged. Unless you rent a house worth ten pounds a year, which Smith notes is "a thing impossible for one who has nothing but his labour to live by," or post a bond the justices find sufficient, which in practice meant thirty pounds, "a security which scarce any man who lives by labour can give."

Parishes then began bribing their own poor to sneak into other parishes and hide for forty days. So, by the 1st of James II, the forty days would count only from the day you handed written notice of your address and family size to a churchwarden. Churchwardens then started quietly accepting the notice and sitting on it. So, by the 3rd of William III, the forty days would count only from the public reading of that notice in church, on a Sunday, immediately after divine service.

Dr. Richard Burn, a justice of the peace who wrote the standard handbook on the subject, explains what the Sunday reading was for:

> It is obvious... that this kind of settlement, by continuing forty days after publication of notice in writing, is very seldom obtained; and the design of the acts is not so much for gaining of settlements, as for the avoiding of them, by persons coming into a parish clandestinely: for the giving of notice is only putting a force upon the parish to remove.

The one legal route by which a poor man could come to belong somewhere new required him to stand up in church and announce himself to the exact people whose only interest was in getting him out before the clock ran. The rule existed to make itself unusable. A man reading his own name into the parish record was starting a countdown he would lose.

Four other routes remained, and each closed itself. Pay parish rates, which required the parish to agree to tax you, and parishes did not do that for a newcomer "who has nothing but his labour to support him." Hold a parish office for a year, same problem. Serve an apprenticeship, which married men do not do. Be hired for a full year, which the law forbade to married servants and which masters had stopped offering in order to avoid granting settlements. Smith's summary: "No independent workman, it is evident, whether labourer or artificer, is likely to gain any new settlement either by apprenticeship or by service."

Then came the workaround, and the workaround is worse. Under the 8th and 9th of William III a man could carry a certificate from his home parish, which obliged any other parish to receive him. But the certificate guaranteed he could never gain a settlement where he went, by any route at all, and it guaranteed his home parish would have to pay to haul him back if he ever needed help. Whether to sign one was left entirely to the churchwardens' mood. Burn, who administered this system, could not keep the distaste out of his sentence: "There is somewhat of hardship in this matter of certificates... by putting it in the power of a parish officer to imprison a man as it were for life."

Somebody once tried to fix that. Burn reports it in a single line, naming no case: "A mandamus was once moved for... to compel the churchwardens and overseers to sign a certificate; but the court of King's Bench rejected the motion as a very strange attempt."

A very strange attempt. On Burn's account, the highest common-law court in England heard the proposition that a poor man might have some claim on being allowed to leave, and found it odd enough to dismiss without argument.

So here is the claim, and it needs one qualification built into it before it goes any further, because a beautiful version of it would be false. How tightly this machinery actually bound is disputed among historians: Norma Landau has argued that removal functioned largely as a registration system parishes used to keep track of migrants rather than a wall that stopped them, while K. D. M. Snell has argued it bit hard and shaped whole labour markets; and the 1795 Removal Act narrowed removal to those who had actually become chargeable, nineteen years after Smith wrote. Smith's "scarce a poor man in England of forty years of age" who has not felt himself "most cruelly oppressed by this ill-contrived law of settlements" is rhetoric, and he was describing the system at its harshest.

The structure, though, is not in dispute, because the structure is just the statutes. And the structure is this. A poor law does move money, a great deal of it: by 1818 English relief ran to something like eight million pounds a year, on the order of two per cent of national income, far and away the biggest thing local government did. But the money was never the part that got legislated. Statute after statute concerned a person, and what it settled about him was where he might stand, what he had to announce, who could send him back, and what he had to own before his presence stopped being a threat. That is not cruelty leaking into an otherwise kind project. It falls straight out of the arithmetic. The instant relief becomes a guarantee owed by a *particular* community to *its own* poor, that community acquires a permanent interest in controlling who counts as its own, and the apparatus of relief becomes an apparatus of registration and removal. Nobody sat down and chose this. Smith called the result "an evident violation of natural liberty and justice," and he was right, and no villain is required to explain it.

Rome had run the same experiment in a different key. The grain dole was never a test of need. It went to a fixed register of citizens, the *plebs frumentaria*, capped by Augustus at around two hundred thousand names, inherited and traded like a title; by the late Empire the entitlement had attached itself to a residence in the city rather than to a person's poverty. Which is why the man in Gibbon has a staircase before he has a loaf. The list came first there too.

Which means the dinner-table argument is about the wrong quantity. The dial is not set to how much. It is set to how much of himself the claimant hands over, and in England what he handed over first was the right to go somewhere else.

## Where the Money Actually Landed

The money does not necessarily reach him either.

By the late eighteenth century many English parishes were topping up wages out of the poor rate, paying labourers according to family size and the price of bread. Marx noticed what that did to the statute book. England had been fixing wages by law for four hundred years, and in 1813 it stopped:

> Finally, in 1813, the laws for the regulation of wages were repealed. They were an absurd anomaly, since the capitalist regulated his factory by his private legislation, and could by the poor rates make up the wage of the agricultural labourer to the indispensable minimum.

An absurd anomaly. There was no longer any point in a law compelling a farmer to pay a living wage, because the parish would cover the gap between what he paid and what a man needed to stay alive. The relief had quietly become a subsidy, and the person collecting it was not the labourer.

This one also needs its qualification at the moment it is stated, because the standard version of it is overcooked. Karl Polanyi made the allowance system into the rot at the heart of English society, and Mark Blaug demolished a lot of that in 1963, showing that the 1834 Royal Commission had built its case on evidence it had effectively cooked, and that allowances in aid of wages were mostly a response to real seasonal unemployment in a depressed agricultural south rather than an engine of demoralization. George Boyer's later work brings back a narrower version: allowance payments were a rational arrangement for farmers who needed a workforce available in August and not in January, and they did shift part of the cost of keeping that workforce alive onto the rates. So the honest form of the claim is the narrow one. Where relief tops up a wage, some of it lands in the employer's pocket, and the size of that share is an empirical question nobody has settled.

Narrow is still enough, because the pattern shows up in the same statutes from a different angle. Marx points out that in four centuries of English wage law "a maximum of wages is dictated by the State, but on no account a minimum." When Samuel Whitbread finally proposed a legal minimum for agricultural labourers in 1796, Pitt opposed it, while conceding, as Marx has it, that the "condition of the poor was cruel." The state would cap what a labourer could be paid and would feed him when the cap left him hungry. It would not raise the cap.

And there is the detail that gives the whole game away, reported to a parliamentary inquirer by a Dr. Hunter about why cottages came without land: "Landlords and farmers... work here hand in hand. A few acres to the cottage would make the labourers too independent."

Too independent. Not too idle. The thing being managed was not the labourer's appetite for work. It was his ability to say no.

Smith had already caught the same machinery from the other end. Weavers thrown out of a decaying trade could not legally move to a growing one, because the Statute of Apprenticeship blocked the change of trade and the Law of Settlements blocked the change of place. So, Smith writes, they "have no other choice but either to come upon the parish, or to work as common labourers, for which, by their habits, they are much worse qualified than for any sort of manufacture that bears any resemblance to their own. They generally, therefore, choose to come upon the parish."

Law makes the man unemployable. Law then relieves him. Then the relief is cited as evidence of his character.

## The Licence and the Brand

The century that invented the poor rate also invented the branding iron for the poor, and the two came out of the same hand.

Marx lays out the sequence, and his rendering wants one caution up front: these were statutes on the books, not a description of ordinary English life. A. L. Beier and Paul Slack have both shown that Tudor vagrancy penalties were enforced unevenly and often not as written, and the worst of the acts below lasted two years before Parliament repealed it. What follows is what the law reached for, which is the thing worth reading, since a legislature reveals itself in what it is willing to authorize.

Under Henry VIII, the Act of 1530: beggars old and unable to work were to receive a beggar's licence, while the able-bodied were "to be tied to the cart-tail and whipped until the blood streamed from their bodies, then to swear an oath to go back to their birthplace or to where they had lived the last three years and to 'put themselves to labour.'" Second offence, under 27 Henry VIII: whipping again, and half the ear cut off. Third: hanged "as a hardened criminal and enemy of the common weal."

Then Edward VI, 1547. Refuse to work and you became the legal slave of whoever informed on you, fed on "bread and water, weak broth and such refuse meat as he thought fit," driven to any task "with whip and chains," branded on the forehead with an S. Idle three days and you were taken to your birthplace, branded on the chest with a V, and set to work in the streets in irons, with "an iron ring round the neck, arms, or legs" so your master could know you again. Parliament repealed this one in 1550, after two years of it. Its last clause, though, let certain poor people be employed by any place willing to feed them and find them work, an arrangement that, Marx notes, "was kept up in England until far into the nineteenth century with the name of 'roundsmen.'"

One statute, with the brand at one end and parish employment in the fine print at the bottom. Set the beggar's licence of 1530 beside it and the whole instrument is visible inside a single generation.

These statutes are obsessed with location rather than laziness. The whipping ends with an oath to return to your birthplace. The V is administered *at* your birthplace. Give a false birthplace and you became the slave of that place for life. The point of the violence is to put people back where they came from, which is exactly the point of the settlement statutes a century later, minus the iron.

Hobbes says the thing outright, in the flat voice of a man who sees no problem:

> And whereas many men, by accident inevitable, become unable to maintain themselves by their labour, they ought not to be left to the charity of private persons, but to be provided for, as far-forth as the necessities of nature require, by the laws of the Commonwealth... But for such as have strong bodies the case is otherwise: they are to be forced to work.

Hobbes is not being callous, and he is not confused. He has grasped that any guarantee of support requires a sorting of the people who ask for it, and that the sorting cannot be voluntary, because if it were the sort would not hold. So the same law that promises bread to the weak must reach for the bodies of the strong. The licence and the brand are two outputs of one machine, and the machine's real product is the classification.

That is why the compassion-versus-discipline picture keeps failing to describe anything. It imagines two rival policies pulling against each other. There is one policy, and it has a hard end and a soft end, and every claimant passes through the part of it that decides which end he gets.

## What the Relief Was Priced In

The price is not money.

John Stuart Mill was, by the standards of 1861, a radical on the franchise. He wanted the vote extended to working men and to women. He wanted it withheld from one group:

> I regard it as required by first principles, that the receipt of parish relief should be a peremptory disqualification for the franchise. He who cannot by his labour suffice for his own support has no claim to the privilege of helping himself to the money of others. By becoming dependent on the remaining members of the community for actual subsistence, he abdicates his claim to equal rights with them in other respects.

He builds out the mechanism with care. Five years clear of the parish books before your name can go on the register. And then: "Those to whom he is indebted for the continuance of his very existence may justly claim the exclusive management of those common concerns, to which he now brings nothing, or less than he takes away."

Mill is not describing a trade-off he regrets. He is describing an exchange he thinks is correct. You took subsistence; you gave up standing. The relief was not free, and it was not paid for in cash.

Gibbon runs the same equation in the opposite direction. He does not say the bread made the plebeians into a mob. He says they became a mob first, and the bread arrived afterward. So long as the people still handed out offices, commands, and provinces with their votes, "their conscious pride alleviated in some measure the hardships of poverty," and their wants were "seasonably supplied by the ambitious liberality of the candidates." They were selling their standing retail, one election at a time, and eating on the proceeds. Then:

> But when the prodigal commons had imprudently alienated not only the use, but the inheritance, of power, they sunk, under the reign of the Cæsars, into a vile and wretched populace.

And so the emperors, in Gibbon's phrase, "studied to relieve the poverty and to amuse the idleness of an innumerable people." That is Gibbon's narrative rather than a clean before-and-after, since the grain laws begin with Gaius Gracchus in 123 BC, while the assemblies still met and still mattered. What changed under the emperors was the title the bread was held under. It stopped being a thing candidates bought and became a thing the state administered, and the administering arrived with a list. Mill takes standing away because relief was given. Gibbon says relief was given because standing was already gone. Both of them are describing one rate of exchange, and the rate is the same in either direction: subsistence trades against standing.

Tiberius had charged that rate in person. Hortalus was no idler. Augustus had given him a million sesterces specifically to marry and raise children so that the house of Hortensius would not die out, and the four boys at the Senate door were what the money had bought. After the speech Tiberius softened a little and voted two hundred thousand sesterces for each son. Then Tacitus closes the scene: "Hortalus said nothing, either from alarm or because even in his reduced fortunes he clung to his hereditary nobility... Nor did Tiberius afterwards show any pity, though the house of Hortensius sank into shameful poverty." He got the money. He was finished.

There is one figure in the tradition who refuses the trade, and he refuses it by changing the title the money is held under. Kant does not treat poor relief as generosity at all. He treats it as a debt:

> For the existence of persons with property in the state implies their submission under it for protection and the provision by the state of what is necessary for their existence; and accordingly the state founds a right upon an obligation on their part to contribute of their means for the preservation of their fellow-citizens.

Property owners are protected by the state; that protection is what makes their property property; the bill for it includes keeping their fellow citizens alive. And so relief "is not to be done merely by voluntary contributions, but by compulsory exactions as state-burdens." Kant even works out the administrative form, preferring current taxation to permanent endowments, because a standing charity grows with the number of poor and starts recruiting, while a legal provision "does not make the profession of poverty a means of gain for the indolent." His reason for choosing taxation over almsgiving is the sentence that matters here: it is "the only one that is conformable to the right of the state, which cannot withdraw its connection from any one who has to live."

Which cannot withdraw its connection. Kant has located the actual variable.

He is not a clean witness for it, and the mess is instructive. The same book sorts citizens into active and passive, and drops into the passive column everyone "compelled to maintain himself not according to his own industry, but as it is arranged by others." Kant does to servants and apprentices roughly what Mill does to paupers. Then he adds three words in a parenthesis: *the state excepted*. He has state employment in mind rather than relief, so carrying it across to the pauper is a step past his text. The parenthesis still shows where he drew the line: dependence on a master costs a man his standing, dependence on the commonwealth under a law does not.

So: if subsistence is a right the claimant holds, it costs him no deference, because a right is not a favour. If it is a gift, it costs him whatever the givers decide, and the givers will find, as Mill did, that they can name a price in citizenship. Same bread, same amount, entirely different transaction, and the difference is the legal title.

A right is not the same thing as a seat, though, and Kant is his own proof of it. His passive citizens hold rights and still do not vote. What the title settles is whether the claimant owes anybody gratitude; whether he gets his standing back is a separate fight, fought later and elsewhere. Britain fought it. The disqualification of relief recipients stood until the Representation of the People Act of 1918 swept it away, in the same years relief began turning into insurance. Parliament did that, not Kant. But the price in standing fell to nothing at exactly the point the money stopped being a favour.

## Hegel's Two Doors

So make it a right, fund it out of taxes, and stop the moralizing. Have we solved it?

Hegel says no, and his reason is the hardest thing in this whole literature.

Once a mass of people has fallen below subsistence, Hegel says, there are exactly two ways to lift them. Door one: hand them the means of living, out of taxes on the rich or out of endowments. Door two: hand them work.

Door one fails on the terms he has already set out. The needy "would receive subsistence directly, not by means of their work, and this would violate the principle of civil society and the feeling of individual independence and self-respect in its individual members."

Door two looks like the obvious answer, and it is the one every serious reformer has reached for, from the Tudor workhouse to the modern jobs programme. Hegel shuts it with one observation:

> In this event the volume of production would be increased, but the evil consists precisely in an excess of production and in the lack of a proportionate number of consumers who are themselves also producers, and thus it is simply intensified by both of the methods (a) and (b) by which it is sought to alleviate it.

As a claim about one moment that is Hegel in 1820 writing about a glut. As a claim about structure it is savage. The reason these people have no work is that the economy is already making more than it can sell, so putting them to work makes more of what cannot be sold. Door two does not relieve the condition. It manufactures the condition, at public expense.

Then the conclusion:

> It hence becomes apparent that despite an excess of wealth civil society is not rich enough, i.e. its own resources are insufficient to check excessive poverty and the creation of a penurious rabble.

Despite an excess of wealth, not rich enough. The scarce thing is not money. A society drowning in goods can still fail to produce the one item it needs, which is a place from which a man can support himself.

Samuel Johnson had bumped into the same wall over dinner, and put it in plainer English:

> It may be questioned, whether there is not some mistake as to the methods of employing the poor, seemingly on a supposition that there is a certain portion of work left undone for want of persons to do it; but if that is otherwise, and all the materials we have are actually worked up... then what is given to the poor, who are to be set at work, must be taken from some who now have it.

Johnson is wrong, and his error has a name. The premise that the quantity of available work is fixed is what economists came to call the lump-of-labour fallacy, and Keynes's whole argument in 1936 was that the fund is not fixed and that public spending can enlarge it. The correction is real, and it is why the twentieth century had a policy answer where the eighteenth did not. Public works do put people to work; the American WPA and India's rural employment guarantee are not illusions. And the objection runs further than volume. A tight labour market moves the terms as well, because when employers are short of hands the power to walk belongs to the worker, which is the thing Dr. Hunter's landlords were guarding against when they kept the acres away from the cottage. Arthur Okun made that case for running an economy hot, and the wage compression at the bottom of the American distribution in 2021 and 2022 is the best recent test of it.

So Hegel wins here conditionally, and it is worth saying which part wins. He never claims the work is finite. He claims that work *available on terms the worker can live on* keeps collapsing faster than the remedies build it, and what survives of that is narrower than he wanted: the terms improve while the demand holds, and the thing that improved them is one more arrangement run on the worker's behalf. Keynes can move the volume, and a hot economy can move the terms. Neither one ends the relationship.

The abundance answer has the same hole in it. *Star Trek*'s Federation is the cleanest statement of the intuition Hegel denies: build a machine that makes anything out of nothing and the problem dissolves. But the thing that makes that world work is not the replicator. It is that nobody in it is anybody's dependent. The writers had to close the ledger, not just fill the pantry. Which is why Hegel's own last move in these paragraphs is not a better relief scheme. It is emigration and foreign markets, a society exporting a problem it cannot hold. The cure is not in the room.

Hegel also records, without a flicker, what Britain had concluded from the experiment. In Britain, "particularly in Scotland, the most direct measure against poverty and especially against the loss of shame and self-respect... as well as against laziness and extravagance, &c., the begetters of the rabble, has turned out to be to leave the poor to their fate and instruct them to beg in the streets."

That is what two hundred years of the best-documented poor law in the world had produced as its most defensible option, according to the century's most systematic philosopher, who was not being sarcastic.

## Back to the Steps

The man on the staircase, then, with his token.

The bread was never the hard part. Rome had grain; the fleets from Africa were the least mysterious thing about the arrangement. The hard part was the ticket, because the ticket is where his name is, and his quarter, and the permission that could be withdrawn. Feeding him was logistics. Placing him was the policy. And every system since has had its ticket: a parish of settlement, a certificate a churchwarden might not sign, a name on the books that keeps you off the register for five years.

Which makes one distinction inside the tradition look much larger than it did.

Aristotle, in the same breath as the leaky cask, says what he thinks should be done instead. Not nothing: "the true friend of the people should see that they be not too poor, for extreme poverty lowers the character of the democracy." What he wants is for public revenue to be saved up and handed over in one piece, in amounts large enough "as may enable them to purchase a little farm, or, at any rate, make a beginning in trade or husbandry." The cask leaks because you keep pouring. Buy the man the farm and there is nothing left to pour into.

Montesquieu, having praised transient aid over permanent institutions, says the same thing about a ruined country: distribute the land to every family in want, "and procure them materials for clearing and cultivating it," and keep doing it as long as there is a man left to receive it.

And Leviticus, which is not usually read as a poor law, turns out to be built entirely on this distinction. If your brother becomes poor and sells himself to you, "you shall not make him to serve as a slave. As a hired servant, and as a temporary resident, he shall be with you; he shall serve with you until the Year of Jubilee: then he shall go out from you, he and his children with him, and shall return to his own family, and to the possession of his fathers." Take no interest from him, "that your brother may live among you." The relief mechanism is not a payment. It is a deadline on the claim, and a return of the man to his own ground.

Water into the cask keeps a man. A farm releases him. One arrangement requires a permanent record of who he is and where he may stand; the other closes the file.

We argue about the size of the payment because the payment is the part we can see. The Romans built ovens and printed tickets, and the tickets outlasted the bread.
